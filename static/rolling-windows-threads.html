Within-thread autocorrelation
Consecutive points on one thread share all but 1 month of data. Curves appear smooth not because returns are stable, but because windows overlap heavily — a 5-year thread point shares ~98% of its data with the adjacent point one month earlier. This is why the haze looks dense and convergent at long horizons: it is an artifact of overlap, not a narrowing of the true outcome distribution.
Across-thread overlap & the “Independent only” toggle
Threads for nearby exit dates also share data. At a 20-year max horizon with ~35 years of history, there are only ~1–2 non-overlapping windows — the nindep bar chart makes this concrete. Toggle Independent only to collapse the haze to exactly those non-overlapping threads. The transition from hundreds of threads to two lonely paths is the most honest summary of what the data actually support.
SE ribbon: what it measures and what it doesn’t
The violet ribbon is ±1 standard error of the mean, computed from the same non-overlapping windows drawn as gold dots — not from the full haze. This self-consistency matters: overlapping windows share ~99% of their data at long horizons, so haze-derived σ is artificially small there. Using σ from the independent set gives an honest spread.

Why the ribbon is roughly flat (not dramatic): For annualized deltas the two horizon effects nearly cancel — longer holds shrink the per-window spread (σindep ∝ 1/√H) but also reduce the count (nindep ∝ T/H), so SE = σ/√nindep ∝ 1/√T — roughly independent of horizon H. The SE is pinned by the total data span T, not by how finely it is sliced. A flat ribbon is therefore correct by construction for annualized quantities. A cumulative (non-annualized) delta would widen; here the effects cancel.

The ribbon vanishes where nindep = 1 (only one independent window — no variance to estimate). This SE measures uncertainty within this single realized history only.
Why annualized deltas compress toward zero at long horizons
A 20-year CAGR is a 20th-root, so even a large terminal-wealth gap between active and passive shrinks to a fraction of a percent per year. The near-zero right edge is partly the metric, not the market — it would look very different on a cumulative (un-annualized) scale where long-horizon gaps spread back out.

More importantly, the 20yr horizon rests on a single non-overlapping window (e.g. 1987→2006 in the Pre-ETF era) — one realized history, not a long-run average. The apparent convergence toward zero is not evidence of equivalence; it is an artifact of annualization plus a sample of one.
Two bands: average uncertainty vs single-window spread
The narrow violet band is ±1 SE of the mean — it answers “where does the average delta sit?” and shrinks as nindep grows. The wide teal band is ±1 σ of the independent windows — it answers “where does a single future window tend to land?” and does not shrink just because more (overlapping) data is added.

The gap between the bands — a factor of √nindep — is exactly how much the overlapping haze is over-claiming precision by treating correlated windows as independent observations. At short horizons (many nindep) the gap is large; at long horizons (few nindep) both bands converge and the wide band itself becomes rough — the honest signal that even the outcome spread is barely estimable.
One history, not a sampling distribution
The entire plot reflects a single realized sequence of market returns. Different regimes (inflation, rate cycles, secular trends) will shift every thread up or down. The SE ribbon captures sampling uncertainty within this history only; it cannot account for regime risk or the fact that the next 20 years may not resemble any sub-period here.
